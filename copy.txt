The snow came early that year, before the harvest was fully in, before
the wood was fully stacked, before anyone in the valley was ready for
what the winter would demand of them. Elena stood at the frost-rimed
window of the farmhouse and watched the fields go white beneath a sky
the color of unpolished pewter, and she understood, in the way one
understands a debt coming due, that this would be a winter people
remembered.

Her grandmother had spoken of winters like this one — winters that
tested not just the body but the will, that asked a person to keep
choosing warmth and light and each other, day after gray day, when
surrender would have been so much easier. The old woman had survived
three such winters in her life. She had told Elena once, over tea gone
cold in its cup, that the secret was never the wood you'd stacked or
the grain you'd stored. The secret was who you let sit beside you in
the dark.

By the second week, the drifts had swallowed the fence line entirely,
and the road to town existed only as a rumor beneath three feet of
snow. Elena rationed the lamp oil and kept the fire low and steady, a
smolder rather than a blaze, because a blaze burns bright and dies
young. She thought often of her grandmother's words as she listened to
the wind test the shutters, searching for any seam it might work
loose. The house held. It always had.
